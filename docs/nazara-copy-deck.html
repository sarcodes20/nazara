The voice

Six rules. Everything in this deck obeys them.

Write it as a wall label. Then take out the adjectives.

  • State, do not persuade. The reader is an architect with a deadline or a principal with a house. Neither has ever been convinced by an adjective. Name the stone, the block, the dimension, the date.
  • Nouns carry the weight. Calcite. Bench. Gangsaw. Bookmatch. Fissure. A sentence built from the trade's own nouns needs almost no modifiers, and reads as expertise rather than as marketing.
  • Short sentences. Then one longer one. The rhythm is a full stop, a full stop, and then a sentence that opens out. Never three long sentences in a row — that is how brochures sound.
  • Say the difficult thing. "We buy perhaps two blocks a year, because most of what is offered as Statuario is not." Candour is the only luxury signal that cannot be counterfeited.
  • Never explain the brand to the reader. No sentence begins "At Nazara, we believe." The website is not a place where the company talks about itself; it is a place where stone is shown properly.
  • Every word earns its place or leaves. If a sentence survives having its adjectives struck out, the adjectives were never load-bearing. Strike them.
Banned outright — inherited from Volume One §01, extended

exquisite · epitome · unparalleled · world-class · one-stop · elevate · curated (as adjective) · timeless · luxurious · opulent · bespoke · journey · unleash · seamless · iconic · legacy · passion · commitment · excellence · state-of-the-art · trusted name · wide range · discover · explore · indulge · transform your space · a touch of · the perfect · nothing short of · we are proud to

Also banned: exclamation marks, rhetorical questions, sentences beginning "Whether you…", any use of "solutions", and the word "premium" in any position.

Preferred, and why
  • seen / kept / left on the bench — the vocabulary of selection, which is the whole business.
  • the library · a viewing · the viewing room · the atelier · the bench — the lexicon fixed in Volume One. Never substituted.
  • block, not batch. slab, not piece. face, not side. drift, not pattern.
  • "We" for the studio, sparingly. "You" almost never — it is the register of a sales page.
  • Numerals written as words in display type ("Forty-one stones"), as figures in every technical context ("41 stones · 6 families").

We wet it.

We turn it.

Stone descriptions

Ten written in full. The eleventh onward follows the template exactly.

A catalogue says what a stone is. A publication says what it does in a room, and what it will not do.

Every entry carries the same five parts: a one-line character, two paragraphs, the technical line, a finish note, and the adjacency rationale. Where a stone has a weakness, the entry says so. That is the entire difference between this library and every competitor's product page.

STONE-01 · /library/makrana-albeta

Makrana Albeta

The house white. Dense, cold to the hand, almost without pattern.

Albeta is the plainest grade Makrana still gives. The field is a warm off-white; the veining, where it appears at all, is a fine grey drift rather than a line. It is a calcite marble, unusually dense and low in porosity, and it takes a honed finish better than a polish — polished, it can look like a photograph of itself.

The quarries are ninety kilometres from this studio, worked on the same bench system since the seventeenth century. The inlay ground of the Taj Mahal came out of them. We state that once and do not repeat it.

Marble · Calcite · Makrana, Nagaur, Rajasthan · 3200 × 1900 mm · 16 / 18 / 20 / 30 mm

Finish. Honed for floors and walls. Leathered where hands will touch it daily. Polished only in low light, and never in a bathroom that gets morning sun.

Behaves similarly. Makrana Kumari, for the same body with movement. Statuario Venato, if the vein must be graphic. Neither is cheaper.

STONE-02 · /library/makrana-kumari

Makrana Kumari

Warmer than Albeta, and it moves.

Kumari carries a soft grey vein that runs in long, unhurried diagonals across the face. Vein-cut it reads as weather. Cross-cut it closes into a still field with the grey pooled rather than drawn. It is the grade to choose when a room needs a white that is not silent.

It is also the grade most often misrepresented, because the name travels further than the bench does. Every slab in this library carries the block it came from and the day it was cut.

Marble · Calcite · Makrana, Nagaur, Rajasthan · 3200 × 1900 mm · 18 / 20 / 30 mm

Finish. Honed. The vein loses its softness under a polish and starts to look printed.

Behaves similarly. Makrana Albeta, for quiet. Calacatta Viola, if the movement should be an event rather than a texture.

STONE-03 · /library/statuario-venato

Statuario Venato

The one everyone asks for, and the one we buy least.

A cold white with a grey vein that is graphic rather than atmospheric — it draws a line across a room and the room follows it. Quarried in the Apuan Alps above Carrara, in a seam that has been worked out and re-entered for two thousand years.

We buy perhaps two blocks a year, because most of what is offered as Statuario is not. If a slab is sold to you without a block number, it is not Statuario. If the vein is identical on every slab in the bundle, it has been printed.

Marble · Calcite · Carrara, Tuscany, Italy · 3200 × 1900 mm · 20 / 30 mm

Finish. Polished, if anywhere. This is the one stone in the library that was made for it.

Behaves similarly. Nothing does, honestly. Makrana Albeta is the closest in body and is a warmer, quieter room.

STONE-04 · /library/calacatta-viola

Calacatta Viola

Oxblood and violet on a white ground. Not a quiet stone.

The veining is brecciated — the marble was broken and healed, and the violet is what filled the fractures. No two slabs agree with each other, which is why it is bought in bookmatched pairs or not at all.

It should be used once in a building and never twice. A second Viola surface in the same house does not double the effect; it halves it.

Marble · Brecciated calcite · Apuan Alps, Italy · 3000 × 1800 mm · 20 mm

Finish. Polished for a vertical plane. Honed for anything horizontal, or the violet goes purple under artificial light.

Behaves similarly. Rosso Levanto, for the same weight in a darker register. Rain Forest Brown, if the drama should come from pattern rather than colour.

STONE-05 · /library/verde-guatemala

Verde Guatemala

Named for a country it does not come from.

A serpentinite: near-black green, threaded with white calcite that runs like a crack pattern rather than a vein. The blocks in this library are quarried in Rajasthan, four hours south of the studio. The name is a nineteenth-century trade convention the industry has never bothered to correct, and we use it because that is what a specification will say.

It is a serpentine, not a true marble, and it does not want water. Specified dry, it is one of the most permanent surfaces we sell. Specified in a wet room, it will disappoint you within two years.

Marble (serpentinite) · Udaipur district, Rajasthan · 2900 × 1750 mm · 18 / 20 mm

Finish. Honed or leathered. Polished serpentine looks like plastic under any light warmer than 3500 K.

Behaves similarly. Nero Marquina, for the same darkness with a cleaner line. Absolute Black, if the green is the problem.

STONE-06 · /library/onice-miele

Onice Miele

The only stone in the library meant to be lit from behind.

Honey onyx is banded calcite laid down by water, layer over layer, in caves. At twenty millimetres it transmits. Backlit, the banding becomes a section drawing of the water that made it, and the wall stops being a wall.

It is not a floor. It is not a worktop. It is soft, it scratches, and it will not survive a kitchen. Used correctly it is the most extraordinary material we hold; used as a surface it is an expensive mistake, and we will say so before you make it.

Onyx · Banded calcite · Sistan, Iran · 2600 × 1600 mm · 20 mm, resin-backed

Finish. Polished. Always backlit at 2700–3000 K, at a minimum of 80 mm from the face, or the fittings will read through.

Behaves similarly. Nothing in the library does. The nearest effect without translucency is Rain Forest Brown, bookmatched.

STONE-07 · /library/nero-marquina

Nero Marquina

Black, with white running through it like chalk on a board.

From Markina in the Basque Country. The ground is a true black limestone-marble and the white is calcite, arriving in irregular fissures that never repeat. A block that promises even veining is a block that has been resined and filled, and we do not buy those.

It marks. Lemon, wine and limescale will all leave a signature on a polished Nero Marquina within a year. That is either unacceptable or it is the point, and the answer is the client's, not ours.

Marble · Bituminous limestone · Markina, Bizkaia, Spain · 3000 × 1800 mm · 20 / 30 mm

Finish. Honed, and sealed twice. Polished only where nothing will be set down.

Behaves similarly. Absolute Black, if the marking is unacceptable. Verde Guatemala, for the same weight with warmth.

STONE-08 · /library/rosso-levanto

Rosso Levanto

Deep oxide red, broken by white, from a coast that no longer exports much of it.

A brecciated serpentinite from Liguria: the red is iron, the white is calcite that filled the breaks. It reads almost black at a distance and opens into colour as you approach, which makes it a stone for corridors and small rooms rather than for large planes.

The good benches are largely worked out. What remains is bought a block at a time and rarely repeats, so a project that needs Rosso Levanto should buy the whole block at the outset. There will not be a matching second order.

Marble (serpentinite) · Levanto, Liguria, Italy · 2800 × 1700 mm · 20 mm

Finish. Polished. Honed, the red goes brown.

Behaves similarly. Calacatta Viola, for the same violence on a light ground. Verde Guatemala, for the same geology in green.

STONE-09 · /library/rain-forest-brown

Rain Forest Brown

Quarried an hour from here. Sold everywhere else as something else.

A dolomitic marble from the Bhainslana belt in Rajasthan. The pattern is dendritic — iron and manganese that migrated through fractures and dried where they stopped. Every slab is a different weather map, and no two blocks from the same bench agree.

It is the stone that first made this region's name and the one most often sold under an invented Italian one. We use the local name because the provenance is the value.

Marble · Dolomitic · Bhainslana, Jaipur district, Rajasthan · 3200 × 1900 mm · 18 / 20 mm

Finish. Honed for floors, leathered for anything vertical that catches light across it.

Behaves similarly. Rain Forest Green, the same bench in a colder register. Calacatta Viola, if the pattern should be violent rather than atmospheric.

STONE-10 · /library/absolute-black

Absolute Black

No pattern at all. That is the specification.

A gabbro from the Deccan, sold as granite, and the only stone in this library bought for its uniformity rather than its drawing. It is hard, it does not mark, and it will outlive the building.

The correct question about Absolute Black is never which quarry but which finish. Polished it is a mirror and shows every fingerprint. Leathered it is a textile. Flamed it is a pavement. The same slab, three different buildings.

Granite (gabbro) · Chimakurthy, Andhra Pradesh · 3200 × 1900 mm · 18 / 20 / 30 mm

Finish. Leathered, in almost every case. Polished only where reflection is the intention.

Behaves similarly. Nero Marquina, if a line through the black is wanted. Nothing else, if it is not.

Before publication

Every technical value above — origins, dimensions, thicknesses, geological classifications and finish behaviour — is written to be accurate and idiomatic, but must be checked line by line against the studio's own test reports, current block inventory and supplier declarations before it goes live. Two claims in particular carry legal and reputational weight and need documentary backing on file: the Makrana provenance statement in STONE-01, and any origin declaration for Iranian onyx in STONE-06, which is subject to sanctions screening in several export markets. Where a value cannot be evidenced, cut the sentence rather than soften it.

Template for stones 11–41
  • Character line — one sentence, maximum fourteen words, set in bold body. It must contain either a physical fact or an admission. Never an adjective alone.
  • Paragraph one — what it is. Geology, colour, how the vein behaves under vein-cut and cross-cut. Two to four sentences.
  • Paragraph two — the difficult thing. The weakness, the scarcity, the misrepresentation in the market, or the room it must not go in. Every entry has one. An entry without one has not been written by someone who has handled the stone.
  • Technical line — family · geology · origin to district level · slab size · thicknesses. Mono, title case, separated by middots.
  • Finish — one or two sentences of instruction, not options. The studio has a view; the page states it.
  • Behaves similarly — two stones and the reason, in technical terms. Never "you may also like". If nothing behaves similarly, say so.

WORK-01 · /works/private-residence-jodhpur · written in full

Private Residence, Jodhpur

2025 · Jodhpur, Rajasthan · 610 m² · Makrana Albeta, vein-cut · Architect [practice] · Photographs [name]

The brief. A courtyard house on the edge of the old city, built to be closed against forty-six degrees for four months of the year. The architect wanted one stone through the whole ground floor, and wanted the vein to continue across every threshold — so the material had to be resolved before the plan was.

We took a single block. NZ-0412 gave eighteen slabs at 3200 × 1900, of which eleven went to this house. They were cut vein-cut, numbered in the order they came off the gangsaw, and dry-laid on the atelier floor in the sequence they would sit in the building. That layout was photographed and sent before anything was crated, and it is the reason the vein runs unbroken from the entrance court through to the stair.

The remaining seven slabs stayed in the library. They are still here, and they still match.

Stones used. Makrana Albeta, vein-cut, honed, 20 mm, block NZ-0412, slabs 1–11.

Next: Hotel, 42 keys · Udaipur

The five remaining briefs, in one paragraph each

Hotel, 42 keys · Udaipur, 2025. Verde Guatemala through the arrival sequence and nowhere else — dark stone at the moment of entry so that the lake, when the corridor opens, arrives as light. Four blocks, held for eleven months while the building caught up.

Apartment, Malabar Hill · Mumbai, 2024. A single backlit Onice Miele plane, 4.2 metres by 2.6, in a room with no window. The banding was chosen for the way it runs horizontally, so that the wall reads as a horizon rather than as a light box.

House by the water · Alibaug, 2024. Rain Forest Brown, leathered, on every vertical surface a hand touches, and nothing on the floors. Salt air rules out most of what a coastal house wants; this stone was quarried ninety minutes inland from a similar climate and has been tested by it.

Flagship, 380 m² · Colaba, 2023. Honed Nero Marquina on the floor, sealed twice, with the acceptance written into the brief that it would mark. Four years on it has, and the client considers the marking the best thing in the room.

Private Residence · Zürich, 2023. Makrana Kumari, shipped nine thousand kilometres to a country with its own quarries, because the client had seen the vein in Jaipur and wanted that bench and no other. Two blocks, one container, eleven weeks.

Rules for every project entry

  • Lead with the constraint, not the glamour. Heat, salt, a windowless room, a client who had already seen the bench. The constraint is what makes it architecture rather than decoration.
  • Name the block. Every project entry cites at least one block number. It is the proof that the manifesto is operational and not a slogan.
  • Credit the photographer, always. On every image and in the credit line. No exceptions, including for images the studio commissioned outright.
  • Never state a budget, a value, or a price per square metre. Not in any market, not on request.
  • No superlatives about the client. "A collector", "a family", "an operator". Never "a leading industrialist" or "one of India's foremost".
  • End on a fact, not a flourish. "The remaining seven slabs stayed in the library. They are still here, and they still match." — the last sentence should be checkable.

PROV-01The word

नज़ारा  ·  نظارہ

Hindi and Urdu, from the Arabic naẓar — the gaze. A nazara is a view. Not scenery: the specific thing you stop walking to look at.

It is a word about the person looking as much as the thing seen. We took it because marble is the only building material whose entire case is optical, and because the work of this studio is not manufacture. It is looking, for long enough, at the right things.

PROV-02The manifesto · verbatim from Volume One, never shortened

Nothing here was designed.
Everything here was chosen.

A block comes out of the hill in the dark. We wet it, rake a light across it, turn it, and look for a long time. Then we take it, or we leave it on the bench. There is no third option, and no amount of money changes the answer.

PROV-03Six quarries · intro, then the mono list beside the drawn map

Six quarries

We buy from six benches, and we have walked all of them.

Not six countries, not fifty. Six specific faces of rock, four of them within a day's drive, each with a foreman we have known for years and a history of what that bench gives in a wet year and a dry one. The library is small because the sourcing is narrow, and the sourcing is narrow on purpose.

PROV-04Makrana · one paragraph beneath a full-bleed image. Nothing more.

Makrana

Ninety kilometres north of this studio, in Nagaur district, there is a marble bench that has been worked since before the Mughals and has never been fully exhausted. The stone from it built the Taj Mahal. It also builds a great many bathrooms in Gurugram, which is the honest and less flattering half of the sentence. What separates one from the other is not the quarry. It is who is standing at the bench on the day the block comes out.

PROV-05The atelier · intro, four finish captions, the dry-lay caption

The atelier

A block becomes a room in four decisions, and none of them are made by a machine.

Orientation. Sequence. Finish. Edge. The gangsaw does the cutting; everything that matters happens before it starts and after it stops.

Polished — Ra 0.2 µm · specular, cold, unforgiving of a bad joint
Honed — Ra 0.8 µm · the studio default; light sits in it rather than on it
Leathered — Ra 3.5 µm · texture without pattern; for hands and for wet feet
Sandblasted — Ra 6.0 µm · exterior and threshold; the only finish with grip

Every bookmatch is dry-laid on the atelier floor, chalked, and photographed before it is crated. If the sequence is wrong, it is wrong here, where it costs a morning — not on site, where it costs a season.

Ra values are house figures for the finishes as produced in this atelier and must be confirmed against current test reports before publication.

ARCH-07Questions we are asked · the FAQ set. Sits in the page's existing utility rhythm, above the trade sign-in.

Questions we are asked.

Can I reserve a block before the client has approved it?
Yes, for twenty-eight days, without payment. After that we will either take a deposit or release it, and we will tell you which before the date rather than after.

Will the second order match the first?
Only if it comes from the same block. Ask us for the whole block at the outset — it is almost always cheaper than a matched re-order, and usually impossible to obtain later at any price.

Do you resin or reinforce?
Onyx and any slab with a structural fissure is resin-backed, and it is stated on the datasheet. We do not fill or dye colour into a stone to make it look like a better grade, and we do not buy blocks that have had it done to them.

Can we see the actual slabs before shipping?
You will see them whether you ask or not. Every bookmatch is dry-laid, chalked and photographed on the atelier floor, and that photograph is sent for approval before anything is crated.

Do you fabricate, or only supply?
Both. Cutting, finishing, edge profiles and dry-lay happen here. Installation is by your contractor, to our detail drawings, and we will attend the first day on site anywhere in India at no charge.

What happens if a slab breaks in transit?
We replace it from the same block if a slab remains, and from the same bench if one does not. If neither is possible we will tell you immediately, because a fortnight's silence is worse than bad news.

Do you work outside India?
Yes. Roughly a fifth of the library leaves the country each year. Freight, crating and documentation are handled here; customs clearance is yours or your agent's.

Is there a minimum order?
No. The smallest thing we have supplied was a single 900 mm threshold. The client came to the viewing for it.

Testimony

Three quotations, placed as pull quotes. Never a carousel.

A brand at this altitude does not run testimonials. It gets quoted.

The format is a single editorial pull quote set in Bodoni at h3, placed inside a Work page between two photographs, attributed to the architect of that project. One per Work page, maximum. No slider, no star rating, no logo wall, no "what our clients say", and nothing on the homepage — a homepage that has to tell you it is trusted is not.

These are specimens, not quotations

The three below are written to establish length, register and subject matter. They are not real quotations and the attributions are invented. Nothing here may be published in any form. Before launch, each must be replaced with words a named person actually said or wrote, with signed permission held on file alongside the project photographs. If real quotations of this quality cannot be obtained, ship the Work pages without the module — an absent pull quote costs nothing, and a fabricated one costs the brand.

Specimen · what a good one does

"They talked us out of the stone we came for, and were right. I have not had that conversation with a supplier before or since."

[Architect] · [Practice] · Private Residence, Jodhpur

Praises a refusal, not a product. The most persuasive thing a client can say about this brand is that it said no.

Specimen · the operational one

"The dry-lay photograph arrived on a Tuesday. We moved two slabs on the drawing and the wall was right on site four weeks later."

[Architect] · [Practice] · Hotel, 42 keys, Udaipur

Specific, procedural, checkable. Reads as a colleague's account rather than an endorsement.

Specimen · the long view

"Four years of marks on the floor. The client thinks it is the best thing in the room. So do I."

[Architect] · [Practice] · Flagship, Colaba

Twenty-two words. Takes the material's supposed weakness and makes it the point.

Rules for obtaining and setting them

  • Ask a question, not for a testimonial. "What surprised you about working with us?" produces usable sentences. "Would you recommend us?" produces a brochure.
  • Twenty-five words or fewer. Longer, and it stops being a quotation and becomes a case study.
  • Never edit for flattery. Trimming for length is permitted; adding an adjective is not.
  • Attribute fully or not at all — name, practice, project. An unattributed quotation is worth less than no quotation.
  • Never quote a private client. Architects and operators only. A principal's words about their own house do not belong on a website.
  • One per page. Never two.

Press, if and when it exists

Where a publication has written about a project, the citation replaces the pull quote and is set identically: the sentence, then the masthead and date in mono. No logo, no "as featured in", no press page. Preferred outlets, in order of usefulness to this audience: Domus, Casabella, Architectural Digest India, Wallpaper*, The Architectural Review, Dezeen. A single sentence in Domus is worth more here than a hundred in the trade press, and should be treated accordingly — one line, set large, and no announcement of the fact.

Alt text

Written for a person who cannot see the photograph, not for a crawler.

Doctrine

  • Describe what is in the frame, in the order the eye would take it. Subject, light, then context.
  • Fifteen words or fewer. Longer alt text is read aloud in full and becomes an obstacle.
  • Never begin "Image of" or "Photo of". The screen reader has already said so.
  • Never repeat the caption. If a mono spec line already states the block number, the alt text does not.
  • Purely decorative images take alt="" — but there are none in this site, because the site has no decoration.
  • No stone name stuffing. The stone is named once, where it is the subject.
